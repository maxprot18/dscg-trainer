# Regulation, competition and compliance

**References:** TFEU art. 101 (anti-competitive agreements), 102 (abuse of a dominant position) and 107 (State aid) ; Regulation (EC) No 1/2003, art. 23 (fines) ; Council Regulation (EC) No 139/2004, art. 1, 7 and 10 (EU Merger Regulation) ; Regulation (EU) 2022/1925 (Digital Markets Act) ; Directive (EU) 2019/1937 (whistleblowers)

**Key issue:** press articles on cartels, Big Tech cases and blocked mergers are classic UE 6 material; you must name the rule infringed (art. 101 or 102), describe merger control and compute a concentration index.

- **Cartels** (art. 101): agreements or concerted practices between competitors, such as price-fixing, market sharing or bid rigging, are prohibited and void. A **leniency programme** grants immunity to the first firm that reports a cartel and reductions to those that cooperate afterwards. Vertical agreements (supplier-distributor) may be exempted when their benefits outweigh restrictions.
- **Abuse of dominance** (art. 102): holding a dominant position (rebuttably presumed from a market share of about 50%, *AKZO* case law) is lawful; abusing it is not — predatory pricing, refusal to supply, **tying** (making the sale of one product conditional on buying another), exclusive dealing, excessive pricing. Winning share through better products is competition on the merits.
- **Fines** can reach 10% of the undertaking's total worldwide turnover in the preceding business year; victims may also claim damages before national courts.
- **Merger control**: concentrations with an EU dimension must be notified to the European Commission ("one-stop shop") and cannot be implemented before clearance (**standstill obligation**; breaching it is "gun-jumping"). EU dimension (main test): combined worldwide turnover above €5,000m and EU-wide turnover of each of at least two parties above €250m, unless each achieves more than two-thirds of its EU turnover in one and the same member state. Substantive test: significant impediment to effective competition (SIEC). Phase I lasts 25 working days; an in-depth Phase II lasts 90 working days (both extendable).
- **Remedies**: structural (divestiture of a business) or behavioural (access commitments); prohibition is rare.
- **Market concentration**: the HHI (Herfindahl-Hirschman Index) is the sum of squared market shares. The Commission is unlikely to find horizontal concerns when the post-merger HHI is below 1,000, or between 1,000 and 2,000 with a delta below 250, or above 2,000 with a delta below 150, absent special circumstances (horizontal merger guidelines 2004/C 31/03, §19-20, still in force in October 2026; a single set of new merger guidelines, published in draft on 30 April 2026, is due to replace them).
- **Ex ante regulation**: the DMA imposes obligations on designated "gatekeepers" of digital platforms (no self-preferencing, interoperability, data portability) without having to prove an abuse first.
- **Compliance**: compliance programmes, codes of conduct, whistleblowing channels, anti-bribery laws (French Sapin II law, UK Bribery Act, US FCPA), anti-money laundering (AML) and sanctions screening. National authorities: Autorité de la concurrence (FR), CMA (UK), FTC and DoJ (US antitrust).

**Key formulas:** HHI = Σ sᵢ² (shares in %) ; delta HHI of a two-firm merger = 2 × s₁ × s₂ ; maximum fine = 10% × worldwide turnover

## Example
Five firms hold 30%, 25%, 20%, 15% and 10% of a market: HHI = 900 + 625 + 400 + 225 + 100 = **2,250**. The fourth and fifth firms merge: delta = 2 × 15 × 10 = **300**, post-merger HHI = 2,550. Above 2,000 with a delta above 150, the Commission will examine the case closely (market investigation, possible remedies).
Separately, a cartel participant with a worldwide turnover of €2,000m faces a fine of at most 10% × 2,000 = **€200m**; as second firm to cooperate it may obtain a reduction, but not immunity.

## Common mistakes
- Treating a 70% market share as an infringement in itself: only the abuse (e.g. tying a cloud service to the software) is unlawful.
- Confusing higher prices for a superior product with excessive pricing: charging more for quality is competition on the merits.
- Believing a company must accept a merger approach from a rival: refusing to merge is not an abuse.
- Closing a notifiable deal before clearance "because it is small": gun-jumping is fined regardless of the deal's effects.

## Key points
- Dominance is not illegal; its abuse is.
- Turnover thresholds decide which authority reviews a deal, not whether it is harmful.
- "Antitrust" (US) = competition law (EU/UK).

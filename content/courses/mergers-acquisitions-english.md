# Mergers, acquisitions and corporate finance

**References:** Directive 2004/25/EC (takeover bids), art. 5 (mandatory bid) and 15 (squeeze-out) ; IFRS 3 Business Combinations, §32 (goodwill) (règlement UE 2023/1803) ; Council Regulation (EC) No 139/2004 (EU Merger Regulation) ; AMF General Regulation, art. 234-2 (30% threshold)

**Key issue:** deal announcements are the most common financial press extracts in UE 6; you must identify the type of deal (friendly, hostile, mandatory), name the defences and compute a premium, an enterprise value or a multiple.

- **Merger** (two companies combine into one) vs **acquisition / takeover** (one company, the **bidder** or acquirer, buys control of another, the **target**). Friendly deals are recommended by the target's board; **hostile** (unsolicited) bids are not, and the board may seek alternatives.
- **Types**: horizontal (competitors: cost synergies, market power), vertical (supplier or customer: securing supply or distribution), conglomerate (unrelated business: diversification).
- **Process**: screening, non-disclosure agreement (NDA), letter of intent or term sheet, **due diligence** (financial, legal, tax, ESG), share purchase agreement (SPA), signing, regulatory clearances (competition, foreign investment), closing, post-merger integration. Between signing and closing, conditions precedent must be satisfied.
- **Public offers**: a **tender offer** is made directly to shareholders; crossing a control threshold triggers a **mandatory bid** (in France, 30% of the capital or voting rights; in the UK, 30% of the voting rights) at an equitable price. A **squeeze-out** lets a bidder holding a very large majority (90% in France) buy out the remaining minority.
- **Consideration**: cash offer, share-for-share exchange (the exchange ratio sets how many bidder shares per target share), or a mix; an **earn-out** makes part of the price contingent on future performance. In a **leveraged buyout** (LBO) a private equity fund finances the deal largely with debt repaid from the target's cash flows.
- **Valuation vocabulary**: equity value (market capitalisation), **enterprise value** (EV = equity value + net debt), multiples (EV/EBITDA, P/E ratio), **control premium** over the undisturbed share price (the price before rumours), cost and revenue **synergies**, **goodwill** (excess of consideration over the fair value of net identifiable assets, IFRS 3).
- **Takeover defences**: poison pill (rights issue that dilutes the bidder), white knight (friendly competing bidder), sale of the "crown jewels" (the assets the bidder wants), staggered board.
- **Corporate finance**: dividend payout ratio, share buyback (*rachat d'actions*), rights issue (*augmentation de capital avec DPS*), spin-off (distribution of a subsidiary's shares), divestment (*cession*).

**Key formulas:** premium = offer price ÷ undisturbed price − 1 ; EV = equity value + net debt ; EV/EBITDA multiple = EV ÷ EBITDA ; P/E = share price ÷ EPS ; goodwill = consideration − fair value of net identifiable assets ; payout ratio = dividends ÷ net income

## Example
Orion Retail has 50 million shares trading at €20 before rumours. Its larger rival offers €26 in cash: premium = 26 ÷ 20 − 1 = **30%**; equity value = 50 × 26 = €1,300m; with net debt of €400m, EV = **€1,700m**, i.e. 10× EBITDA of €170m. Net income of €80m gives EPS of €1.60 and a P/E at the offer price of 16.25.
If the fair value of Orion's net identifiable assets is €900m, the acquirer records goodwill of 1,300 − 900 = **€400m** (IFRS 3). The board rejects the "unsolicited" bid as undervaluing the company and talks to a private equity fund: the bid is hostile and the fund is a potential white knight.

## Common mistakes
- Calling an unsolicited bid "friendly" because a competing offer may follow: friendly means recommended by the target's board.
- Describing the white knight as a poison pill: the pill is a dilutive mechanism, the knight is a rival friendly bidder.
- Confusing the mandatory bid (triggered by crossing 30%) with the squeeze-out (buying out minorities after reaching about 90%).
- Comparing an EV/EBITDA multiple with a P/E: the first values the whole business, the second only the equity.

## Key points
- Enterprise value is the value of the whole business (for all capital providers); equity value is what shareholders receive.
- Many announced synergies are never delivered: integration risk is the main cause of value destruction.
- "Merger" in everyday English often describes what is legally an acquisition.

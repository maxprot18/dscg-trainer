# Strategy and business models

**References:** Porter, Competitive Strategy (1980) and Competitive Advantage (1985) ; Ansoff, "Strategies for Diversification" (1957) ; Boston Consulting Group growth-share matrix (Henderson, 1970) ; Osterwalder and Pigneur, Business Model Generation (2010)

**Key issue:** UE 6 cases describe a company's environment or a new venture and ask you to apply a framework (five forces, Ansoff, BCG, business model canvas) in English; the vocabulary of each model must be exact, and the classification justified by the facts given.

- **External analysis**: PESTEL (political, economic, social, technological, environmental, legal); **Porter's five forces**: rivalry among existing competitors, threat of new entrants (barriers to entry), threat of substitutes (a different product meeting the same need: video calls or high-speed rail for a short flight), bargaining power of buyers, bargaining power of suppliers. Strong forces mean low industry profitability.
- **Internal analysis**: resources and **core competences** (valuable, rare, hard to imitate), value chain (primary activities such as operations and marketing, support activities such as HR and IT), SWOT (strengths and weaknesses are internal, opportunities and threats are external).
- **Generic strategies (Porter)**: cost leadership (lowest cost in the industry, prices at or below rivals), differentiation (unique attributes justifying a premium price), focus (cost focus or differentiation focus on a niche). Being "stuck in the middle" is the classic trap; cost leadership and premium pricing are not naturally consistent.
- **Ansoff matrix**: market penetration (existing products, existing markets), market development (existing products, new markets), product development (new products, existing markets), diversification (new products, new markets: the riskiest option).
- **BCG matrix**: market growth rate vs **relative market share** (own share ÷ share of the largest competitor). Stars (high growth, high share), cash cows (low growth, high share: generate cash), question marks (high growth, low share: need cash), dogs (low growth, low share). Cash cows fund question marks that may become stars.
- **Growth modes**: organic growth, mergers and acquisitions, strategic alliances and joint ventures; vertical integration (upstream or downstream) vs horizontal integration; outsourcing of non-core activities.
- **Business model canvas** (9 building blocks): customer segments, value proposition, channels, customer relationships, revenue streams, key resources, key activities, key partnerships, cost structure.
- **Business model types**: subscription, freemium (free basic version, a fraction of users pay for premium), platform / two-sided marketplace with **network effects** (direct: more users attract users; indirect: more sellers attract buyers and vice versa), razor-and-blades (cheap base product, margin on consumables), pay-per-use.

**Key formula:** relative market share = own market share ÷ market share of the largest competitor (above 1 = market leader)

## Example
Nordlys, a Scandinavian appliance maker, has two business units. Dishwashers: market growth 2% a year, Nordlys 35% share, largest rival 20% → relative share 1.75, a **cash cow**. Heat pumps: market growth 12%, Nordlys 20%, leader 25% → relative share 0.8, a **question mark** that needs the dishwashers' cash to gain share.
Nordlys then considers selling its heat pumps to commercial buildings in Germany, a segment and country where it is absent: existing product, new market → **market development** (Ansoff). Launching a home-energy-management software for its existing households would be product development; entering electric bikes would be diversification.

## Common mistakes
- Reading high-speed rail or video conferencing as "new entrants" to the airline industry: they are substitutes, a different product for the same need.
- Computing relative market share against the whole market (20% → 0.2) instead of against the largest competitor (20 ÷ 25 = 0.8).
- Describing razor-and-blades as earning the margin on the expensive initial product: the margin is on the consumables.
- Treating cost leadership and premium pricing as consistent: low costs allow low prices or high margins at market price, not a premium.

## Key points
- Relative market share uses the largest competitor, not the market total.
- Diversification is the riskiest Ansoff option because both the product and the market are new.
- Network effects: the service becomes more valuable as more users join.

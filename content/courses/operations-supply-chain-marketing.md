# Operations, supply chain and marketing

**References:** Ohno, Toyota Production System (1988) ; Womack and Jones, Lean Thinking (1996) ; McCarthy (1960) and Booms and Bitner (1981) on the marketing mix ; Kotler and Keller, Marketing Management

**Key issue:** UE 6 extracts from annual reports or trade press describe supply chain disruptions, inventory policies or product launches; you must use the vocabulary accurately and compute an operations or marketing indicator (DIO, churn, break-even).

- **Supply chain** (*chaîne logistique*): procurement/purchasing (*achats*), production, warehousing, distribution, reverse logistics (returns, recycling). Upstream = suppliers, downstream = customers. The **bullwhip effect** amplifies small demand changes up the chain.
- **Operations vocabulary**: lead time (*délai d'approvisionnement*), throughput, capacity utilisation, bottleneck (*goulet d'étranglement*: the step that limits total output), safety stock (*stock de sécurité*), reorder point, stock-out (*rupture de stock*), backlog (orders not yet fulfilled), on-time delivery rate.
- **Lean management**: eliminate waste (*gaspillage*; Ohno's seven wastes: overproduction, waiting, transport, over-processing, inventory, motion, defects), **just-in-time** (JIT: produce or receive only what is needed, when needed, which lowers inventories but increases exposure to disruptions), kaizen (continuous improvement), total quality management, Six Sigma (defect reduction).
- **Resilience**: nearshoring/reshoring, dual sourcing (two suppliers for a critical component), buffer stocks, supplier risk mapping; the trade-off is cost versus security of supply.
- **Marketing mix**: 4Ps (product, price, place = distribution channels, promotion = communication), extended to 7Ps for services (people, process, physical evidence). Selling only online and through selected pharmacies is a **place** decision (selective distribution).
- **STP**: segmentation (dividing the market by needs or behaviour), targeting (choosing segments), positioning (the place the brand occupies in customers' minds); unique selling proposition (USP), brand equity.
- **Pricing**: penetration pricing (low launch price to gain share quickly) vs price skimming (high launch price for early adopters, lowered later); premium pricing, dynamic pricing, cost-plus pricing. **Break-even**: the volume at which contribution covers fixed costs.
- **Customer metrics**: customer acquisition cost (CAC), customer lifetime value (CLV, which must exceed CAC), churn rate (share of customers lost over a period), retention rate = 1 − churn, conversion rate, net promoter score (NPS = % promoters − % detractors).

**Key formulas:** inventory turnover = cost of sales ÷ average inventory ; days inventory outstanding (DIO) = average inventory ÷ cost of sales × 365 ; churn rate = customers lost ÷ customers at the start of the period ; break-even volume = fixed costs ÷ (unit price − unit variable cost)

## Example
Fjord Foods plc (year to 31 December, £k): revenue 12,000, cost of sales 9,125, inventories 1,400 at the start and 1,600 at the end. Average inventory = 1,500; turnover = 9,125 ÷ 1,500 = 6.08 times; DIO = 1,500 ÷ 9,125 × 365 = **60 days** (using revenue would give 45.6 days, wrong because inventories are at cost).
Its subscription meal-kit service had 5,000 customers on 1 January and lost 400 during the year: churn = **8%**, retention 92%. A new kit sells at £40 with a variable cost of £25 and fixed costs of £240k a year: break-even = 240,000 ÷ 15 = **16,000 kits**, i.e. £640k of revenue.

## Common mistakes
- Assigning a distribution decision (own website plus selected pharmacies) to promotion or product: it is place.
- Defining price skimming as a low launch price: that is penetration pricing; skimming starts high.
- Computing DIO on revenue rather than cost of sales, which understates the days.
- Treating JIT as risk-free: lower stocks mean a supplier failure stops production.

## Key points
- "Place" in the 4Ps means distribution, not the shop's location only.
- Inventory ratios use cost of sales (not revenue) when inventories are valued at cost.
- JIT reduces holding costs but makes the firm vulnerable to supplier failures.

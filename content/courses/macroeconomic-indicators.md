# Macroeconomic indicators: growth, inflation, unemployment

**References:** European System of Accounts ESA 2010 (Regulation (EU) No 549/2013) ; ILO definition of unemployment (13th ICLS resolution, 1982) ; Eurostat HICP methodology

**Key issue:** the UE 6 exam gives a short economic text or a central bank bulletin in English and asks you to read it correctly (growth, inflation, unemployment) and to compute one figure from it, usually a real growth rate or an unemployment rate.

- **GDP** (gross domestic product, *PIB*): market value of all final goods and services produced within a country over a period. Three approaches give the same total: output (sum of value added, which excludes intermediate consumption), expenditure (C + I + G + X − M) and income (wages + gross operating surplus + taxes on production less subsidies).
- **Nominal GDP** is measured at current prices; **real GDP** at constant prices (volume). The **GDP deflator** (nominal ÷ real × 100) measures the price change of everything produced, whereas the CPI measures the price change of what households consume.
- **Recession**: the usual rule of thumb is two consecutive quarters of negative real GDP growth; some bodies (the NBER in the US) use broader criteria such as employment and income.
- **Inflation**: sustained rise in the general price level, measured by a consumer price index (CPI; in the euro area the HICP, *IPCH*). **Headline inflation** covers the whole basket; **core inflation** excludes volatile items (energy, food). A fall in headline inflation driven by energy prices while core inflation stays high means underlying price pressures persist.
- **Disinflation** = inflation is falling but still positive; **deflation** = prices are falling (negative inflation); **stagflation** = high inflation combined with stagnant growth and rising unemployment.
- **Unemployment rate** (ILO): unemployed ÷ labour force. An unemployed person has no work, is available and is actively looking for a job; a discouraged worker who has stopped searching is inactive, not unemployed. The labour force (*population active*) = employed + unemployed; the participation rate = labour force ÷ working-age population.
- **Output gap**: actual GDP minus potential GDP (in % of potential); a negative gap signals spare capacity and weak inflationary pressure, a positive gap signals overheating.

**Key formulas:** real growth = (1 + nominal growth) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1 ≈ nominal growth − inflation ; unemployment rate = unemployed ÷ (employed + unemployed)

## Example
Nominal GDP of Nordia rises from €2,400bn to €2,544bn (+6.0%) while the GDP deflator moves from 110 to 113.3 (+3.0%). Real growth = 1.06 ÷ 1.03 − 1 = **2.91%** (the approximation 6 − 3 = 3% overstates it by 0.09 point).
The same year, Nordia has 30 million people in work, 2 million unemployed (ILO definition) and 10 million inactive people of working age. Labour force = 32 million; unemployment rate = 2 ÷ 32 = **6.25%**, not 2 ÷ 42 = 4.8%.

## Common mistakes
- Adding imports in the expenditure approach: imports are deducted (X − M), because they are produced abroad.
- Reading a sharp fall in inflation (6% to 3%) as deflation: prices still rise, this is disinflation.
- Dividing the unemployed by the working-age population instead of the labour force.
- Summing firms' turnover in the output approach: GDP sums value added, which is net of intermediate consumption, otherwise inputs would be counted twice.

## Key points
- Disinflation is not deflation: prices still rise, only more slowly.
- The unemployment rate is calculated on the labour force, not on the total working-age population.
- The approximation "nominal − inflation" becomes inaccurate when rates are high: use the exact formula.
- GDP measures production on the national territory; GNI (*RNB*) adds net income received from abroad.

# Banking and financial markets

**References:** Basel III framework (BCBS), implemented in the EU by Regulation (EU) No 575/2013 (CRR, art. 92) and Directive 2013/36/EU (CRD) ; Directive 2014/65/EU (MiFID II) ; Regulation (EU) No 596/2014 (Market Abuse Regulation, art. 14-15) ; Regulation (EU) No 1024/2013 (Single Supervisory Mechanism)

**Key issue:** financial news in English (rate moves, bank stress, bond sell-offs) requires exact vocabulary; the exam typically asks you to compute a bond yield or a capital ratio and to explain why prices and yields move in opposite directions.

- **Banks**: commercial (retail) banks take deposits and grant loans, earning a **net interest margin** (interest received − interest paid); investment banks advise on M&A, underwrite securities issues and trade on markets. Banks transform short-term deposits into long-term loans, which exposes them to runs and liquidity crises.
- **Prudential regulation (Basel III)**: banks hold capital against risk-weighted assets (RWA): a government bond is weighted less than an unsecured corporate loan. **CET1 ratio** = Common Equity Tier 1 capital ÷ RWA; minimum 4.5%, plus the 2.5% capital conservation buffer and, where applicable, countercyclical and systemic buffers. The **leverage ratio** (Tier 1 ÷ total exposure, at least 3%) is a backstop that ignores risk weights. Liquidity ratios: **LCR** (30-day stress) and **NSFR** (stable funding over one year), each at least 100%.
- **Supervision**: in the euro area, the ECB directly supervises significant banks (Single Supervisory Mechanism); other authorities include the EBA, ESMA, the ACPR and AMF (France), the PRA and FCA (UK), the Fed and SEC (US).
- **Markets**: money market (short-term funding) vs capital markets (equity and debt). **Primary market** = issue of new securities (an IPO, *introduction en bourse*, or a bond issue, with underwriting by an investment bank); **secondary market** = trading of existing securities between investors, which gives them liquidity.
- **Bonds**: face (par) value, coupon, maturity, yield. Bond prices and yields move in **opposite directions**: when market rates rise, existing fixed-rate bond prices fall so that their yield matches the new rates. Credit ratings: **investment grade** (BBB−/Baa3 or above) vs **high yield** ("junk"); the **spread** over the risk-free rate rewards credit risk.
- **Yield curve**: yields by maturity; an **inverted** curve (short rates above long rates) is often read as a recession signal.
- **Derivatives**: futures, forwards, options and swaps, used for **hedging** (*couverture*) or speculation; traded on exchanges or over the counter (OTC).
- **Market abuse**: insider dealing (trading on inside information), unlawful disclosure and market manipulation are prohibited; issuers must disclose inside information promptly.

**Key formulas:** current yield = annual coupon ÷ market price ; one-year yield to maturity = (coupon + face value) ÷ price − 1 ; CET1 ratio = CET1 capital ÷ RWA ; leverage ratio = Tier 1 capital ÷ total exposure

## Example
A corporate bond (face value €1,000, 3% annual coupon) matures in exactly one year and trades at €985. Current yield = 30 ÷ 985 = 3.05%; yield to maturity = (30 + 1,000) ÷ 985 − 1 = **4.57%**: below par, the investor earns the coupon plus a €15 capital gain. If market rates then fall, the price rises towards and above par.
A bank has CET1 capital of €9bn, RWA of €75bn and total assets of €300bn. CET1 ratio = 9 ÷ 75 = **12%**, well above 4.5% + 2.5% = 7%; its leverage ratio is about 9 ÷ 300 = 3%, right at the Basel floor, so the binding constraint is leverage, not risk-weighted capital.

## Common mistakes
- Computing the CET1 ratio on total assets (9 ÷ 300 = 3%) and concluding the bank is undercapitalised: the ratio uses risk-weighted assets.
- Believing bond prices rise when market rates rise: they fall.
- Inventing a 15% CET1 minimum: the requirement is 4.5% plus buffers, typically 10 to 12% in practice for large banks.
- Confusing shares (equity, residual claim) with bonds (debt, fixed claim).

## Key points
- A bond trading below par yields more than its coupon rate.
- The CET1 ratio is calculated on risk-weighted assets, not on total assets (the leverage ratio uses total exposure).
- "Share" (UK) = "stock" (US); "shares" ≠ "bonds" (debt securities, *obligations*).

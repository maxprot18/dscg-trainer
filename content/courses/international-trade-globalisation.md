# International trade and globalisation

**References:** GATT 1994, art. I (most-favoured-nation treatment) and art. III (national treatment) — WTO agreements ; TFEU art. 206-207 (common commercial policy) ; IMF Balance of Payments Manual (BPM6) ; Regulation (EU) 2023/956 (CBAM)

**Key issue:** trade disputes, tariffs and supply chain relocation are recurring topics of the UE 6 texts; you must use the vocabulary accurately, read a trade balance and apply comparative advantage to a small numerical case.

- **Comparative advantage** (Ricardo): a country gains from specialising in the good it produces at the lowest **opportunity cost** (what it gives up of the other good), even if it has no **absolute advantage** in any good. Both countries gain when they trade at a price between their two opportunity costs.
- **Trade barriers**: tariffs (customs duties), quotas, non-tariff barriers (standards, licences), export subsidies. **Anti-dumping duties** target imports sold below normal value. Tariffs raise domestic prices, protect domestic producers at the expense of consumers and invite retaliation (trade wars).
- **WTO principles**: most-favoured-nation (a concession granted to one member is granted to all) and national treatment (no discrimination between imported and domestic goods once on the market). Disputes go to the WTO dispute settlement body (panels still rule, but the Appellate Body has been unable to hear appeals since December 2019).
- **Levels of integration**: free trade area (no internal tariffs, each member keeps its own external tariff) → customs union (plus a common external tariff; the EU is one) → single market (free movement of goods, services, capital and people) → monetary union (single currency).
- **Balance of payments**: current account (goods, services, primary and secondary income), capital account, financial account. A **trade deficit** means imports of goods exceed exports; the balance of goods and services adds the services balance. By construction, a current account deficit is matched by net capital inflows.
- **Exchange rates**: an appreciation of the domestic currency makes exports dearer for foreign buyers and imports cheaper; a depreciation does the reverse but imports inflation. **Terms of trade** = export price index ÷ import price index × 100: above 100, a given volume of exports buys more imports.
- **Globalisation trends**: global value chains, offshoring, reshoring / nearshoring, "friend-shoring" and de-risking of supply chains. The EU **carbon border adjustment mechanism** (CBAM) puts a carbon price on certain imports (steel, cement, aluminium, fertilisers, electricity, hydrogen) so that imported goods bear the same carbon cost as EU production.

**Key formulas:** opportunity cost of 1 unit of A = units of B given up ; trade balance = exports of goods − imports of goods ; terms of trade = export price index ÷ import price index × 100

## Example
With one hour of labour, Alpha produces 6 cars or 12 tonnes of wheat; Beta produces 2 cars or 8 tonnes. Alpha is more productive in both goods (absolute advantage), but the opportunity cost of one car is 2 tonnes in Alpha and 4 tonnes in Beta: Alpha specialises in **cars**, Beta in **wheat**, and any exchange rate between 2 and 4 tonnes per car benefits both.
Reading a balance: goods exports €420bn, goods imports €465bn, services surplus €60bn. Trade balance = **−€45bn** (deficit); balance of goods and services = +€15bn. Terms of trade with export prices at 112 and import prices at 105: 112 ÷ 105 × 100 = **106.7**.

## Common mistakes
- Thinking the more productive country should produce everything: specialisation follows comparative, not absolute, advantage.
- Defining a customs union by free movement of workers or a single currency: its distinctive feature is the common external tariff.
- Believing an appreciation of the euro makes euro-area exports cheaper abroad: it makes them dearer.
- Reading a current account deficit as a loss: it is financed by capital inflows and may reflect strong investment.

## Key points
- Free trade area ≠ customs union: only the latter has a common external tariff.
- Trade policy is an exclusive competence of the EU: member states do not negotiate trade agreements alone.
- A current account deficit is not necessarily "bad": it is financed by capital inflows (financial account).
